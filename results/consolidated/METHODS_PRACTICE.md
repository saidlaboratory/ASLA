# Methods practice: verify that the measurement can see what you think it sees

Generated by `scripts/render_consolidated_findings.py` from committed JSON.

Eight times in this project a signal or a null result turned out to be an artifact of measuring the wrong quantity rather than a property of the system. In every case the error was invisible to the surrounding tests, and in 6 of the 8 the wrong reading was the *comfortable* one. The practice that caught them is a single question, asked before believing any null or any signal:

> **Can this measurement, as constructed, detect the thing I am claiming it detects or rules out?**

## Instance 1: a correction that mathematically could not correct anything

Down-weighting a serially correlated run by one constant factor changes fitted parameters by at most **1.3e-03** relative, because a uniform weight cancels in the least-squares normal equations. A weight that varies across groups changes them by **1.0e+00**, a factor of **745x** larger. The first implementation of the checkpoint correlation correction used a per-run constant and was therefore a no-op; it ran, produced plausible output, and corrected nothing.

**How it was caught:** the naive and corrected arms returned *identical* results, which was implausible given a measured autocorrelation of 0.43-0.80.

## Instance 2: comparing summary rates instead of the thing that changed

The same correction was then judged by comparing pairwise mis-selection rates, which coincided at 4.00% for both weightings. That read as a second no-op. It was not: the correction shifts projected values by up to **3.27e-02** and changes the induced ordering (True). Two genuinely different fits can land on the same summary rate by coincidence.

**How it was caught:** asking what the correction was supposed to change (the fit) rather than what was convenient to compare (the rate).

## Instance 3: a gate that found an error in already-published work

A `phi = 0` limit gate was installed to guard a new variance model. On its first run it failed at ratio **2.06** and stopped the script. The cause was not in the new work: the analytic side squared a *mean* of relative standard errors while the numeric side averaged their *squares*. With a noise coefficient of variation near 1.0 those differ by a factor of ~2 (Jensen). The numeric estimator was correct and the analytic constant - already reported in the two-parameter theory - was the loose one. After correction the gate passed at exactly 1.0000.

**How it was caught:** the gate compared two routes to the same quantity and was made a hard stop that wrote its result before any downstream number existed.

## Instance 4: principal components of numerical noise

A synthetic control was built to check whether interventions differing in curvature produce a 'scaled' residual signature. It reported that even the *level* family showed one, which would have put a confident and wrong mechanism into the writeup. The control was invalid: its truths were exact power laws, so a power-law fit left residuals at **~1e-16** - the analysis was a principal-component decomposition of floating-point noise.

Rebuilt with injected misspecification, the control reverses the conclusion: with a shared misspecification amplitude, cancellation stays at 0.000-0.002 across a true exponent spread of 0 to 0.6, i.e. curvature has no effect at all.

**How it was caught:** the control contradicted a result that was measured directly, so the control itself was checked; its residual magnitudes were nine orders of magnitude below the real data's.

## Instance 5: a ratio of scales built from a mean of standard errors

The headline statement that misspecification dominates was reported as a residual scatter of **4.22x** the seed standard error, a **17.8x** variance inflation. Both were wrong, and both had reached three committed documents as the opening premise of this section. The numerator was a dof-corrected residual *scale*; the denominator pooled the per-cell standard errors as `mean(se)`. A ratio of scales requires `sqrt(mean(se^2))`, larger by 1.46x here, so the ratio was inflated. The corrected values, now computed rather than asserted, are **2.90x** and **8.40x**.

This is the same Jensen-gap class as instance 3, which had already been found and fixed elsewhere in the codebase - the pattern recurred in a different file because the fix was local to the site, not to the habit. The figures were also *hardcoded literals* in the results JSON, with no computation behind them, which is what let a plausible-looking number survive: `4.22^2 = 17.81` is internally consistent, so the pair passed any cross-check that did not go back to the data.

**How it was caught:** an unrelated audit recomputed the ratio from the same design and got 2.90x. The discrepancy forced a reconstruction of the original arithmetic, which reproduced 4.221 exactly from the mismatched pooling.

**What changed as a result:** the direction of every conclusion is unaffected - misspecification still dominates seed noise by a wide margin, and no verdict in any pre-registration turns on the magnitude. What changed is that the number is now derived, carries its inputs and design label in the JSON, and cannot drift again.

## Instance 6: the same error again, in the file the fix was compared against

A sweep for the *pattern* rather than for literals found it a third time. `run_theory_check.py` computed its log-space noise as `mean(relative_se)` and then squared it in every consumer: the expected-flip-rate model forms `2*sigma^2*leverage`, and the variance diagnosis forms `sigma^2*leverage`. Squaring a linear mean of scales understates `sigma^2` by the squared coefficient of variation of those scales, here **2.06x**.

That factor is the point. Instance 3 was a `phi = 0` gate that failed at a ratio of **2.06** for exactly this reason, and was fixed in `run_theory_v2.py`. The same error sat undisturbed in `run_theory_check.py` --- which is the two-parameter theory that v2 compares *itself against*. A literal sweep would never have found it, because nothing was typed: the wrong aggregation was computed correctly.

**How it was caught:** searching for the mathematical shape (a linear aggregate over a scale entering a nonlinear expression) across every site in the codebase, rather than for wrong-looking numbers.

**What changed, stated as a weakened claim.** No pre-registered verdict moved --- T1 is sigma-free, and T2 and T3 compare a measured quantity against a committed band. The *diagnosis* weakened: the ratio of empirical to theoretical projection variance falls to **1.55** from a previously reported 3.20, and the implied gap-standard-deviation understatement to **1.25** from 1.79. Roughly half of what we had attributed to the unmodelled third parameter was our own pooling error --- we were partly explaining our own bug. The omission is still real and still points the right way, but it no longer accounts for the T2 miss on its own.

## Instance 7: a test with no power, used to refute a claim

A pre-registered scoring rule restricted a comparison to pairs whose reference ordering was statistically resolved. On that subset two selection rules showed an identical error rate, and the pre-registration had committed in advance to reading a difference below a stated threshold as evidence that the original effect was reference noise. The reading was wrong: on that subset *both rules made zero errors*, so the test could not have detected any difference. A tie between two methods that never err is not evidence they are equivalent.

**How it was caught:** sweeping the determination threshold instead of reporting one cut. The gap was zero only across thresholds where the error count was zero, and reappeared at the first threshold admitting any error. An equivalence bound made it explicit --- the rule of three on a zero-event count rules out only differences above 1.22 percentage points, a bound set by subset size rather than by observed agreement.

**The general form:** before reading a null as evidence of no effect, check that the comparison contained enough events to have shown one. This is the power question, and a pre-registered threshold does not substitute for it.

## Instance 8: a headline count produced by uncommitted state

The project's decomposition rested on 16 projection ranking errors against 4 for the baseline. Those counts do not regenerate. At the commit that wrote them, running that commit's own detectors on byte-identical input data, the counts are 10 and 3. The detector module is unchanged between that commit and now, and the input hashes match exactly, so neither code nor data can explain the difference: the shipped file was produced by working state that was never committed.

**How it was caught:** an unrelated re-scoring produced 10 and 3 and did not match the shipped file. The detection path was then mechanical --- run the committed detectors at HEAD, run them again in a worktree at the writing commit, and hash the input data at both ends. Two independent implementations agreeing with each other and disagreeing with the file is what makes the conclusion safe.

**Why it is the most consequential of the eight:** every downstream number inherited it, including an external reviewer's own arithmetic about what the counts implied. The direction of the finding survived; the magnitudes did not. A results file is not evidence unless it can be regenerated from committed state, and nothing in the project checked that until now.

## A numerical fingerprint for this class

Instances 3, 5 and 6 are one defect: a quantity aggregated in one space and consumed in another, where the transform between them is nonlinear. It has a detectable signature. When residual standard errors have a coefficient of variation near 1 --- which is ordinary for seed noise across a compute ladder --- squaring their mean understates the mean of their squares by about a factor of two. Both instance 3 and instance 6 presented as a discrepancy of **2.06x**.

So: **any unexplained ratio landing near 2, in a context where a mean and a square are both involved, is a candidate for this class.** That is a cheaper test than re-deriving the algebra, and it is what turns 8 anecdotes into something a reader can apply. The general rule underneath it: `mean(se)` is the right summary for *a typical standard error* and the wrong one for *the denominator of a ratio of scales*, which needs `sqrt(mean(se^2))`; aggregating in one space and consuming in another is valid only where the transform is linear.

## The two tests that would have caught all six

Every instance failed one of two questions, and between them they cover the set:

1. **Can the measurement see what the correction is supposed to change?** Instance 1's correction was a mathematical no-op; instance 2 compared a summary rate instead of the fit that moved; instance 4 ran a decomposition on floating-point noise. In each the apparatus could not have detected the effect it was asked about, whatever the truth.
2. **Can the number be reconstructed from shipped inputs, rather than merely being self-consistent?** Instance 5's pair satisfied every internal check --- 4.22 squared is 17.81 --- and was still wrong, because both halves were typed together. Instances 3 and 6 were computed correctly from a quantity aggregated in the wrong space, which self-consistency also cannot see.

The second question is the one worth generalising. Self-consistency tests what a number agrees with; reconstructibility tests what it came from. Only the latter fails when an input is right and its aggregation is wrong.

## What the 8 have in common

| # | The comfortable reading | What was actually true | What exposed it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | the correction is applied | a uniform weight cancels; nothing changed | implausible exact agreement |
| 2 | the correction does nothing | fits and orderings changed; rates coincided | comparing the changed object |
| 3 | the new model is miscalibrated | a shipped constant was wrong | a hard gate on a known limit |
| 4 | curvature causes the signature | PCA of 1e-16 noise | residual magnitude check |
| 5 | misspecification is 4.22x seed noise | 2.90x; a Jensen-biased denominator | independent recomputation |
| 6 | the omitted third parameter explains the misses | half of it was our own pooling error | a sweep for the pattern, not for literals |

**Status is not uniform, and the uneven version is the stronger one.** Instances 1-4 and 6 were caught before any claim built on them left the repository. Instance 5 was not: it propagated into five committed documents and a paper draft as the opening premise of a headline section, and it is the reason this is a finding rather than a note on good hygiene. A defect class that is always caught in time is a story about diligence; one that reaches a draft is a story about why the controls have to be structural.

6 of the 8 (1, 2, 4, 5, 6) would have produced a *publishable-looking* claim, and instance 5 overstated the project's own headline by 1.5x in scatter and 2.1x in variance. Only instance 3 was uncomfortable at the time, and it is the one that found a defect in work already reported. The asymmetry is the point: artifacts that flatter the hypothesis are the ones least likely to be questioned. Instances 3, 5 and 6 are the same error in three places, which is the sharper lesson - fixing an instance is not the same as fixing the habit, and the third recurrence was in the very file the second fix was compared against.

**Operationally, in this codebase:** limit gates that write their verdict before downstream numbers exist; controls that assert their own residuals are real; corrections tested for a measurable effect on the object they modify rather than on a summary statistic; a loader that reads reported numbers from their source JSON by key path, with a lint that rejects numeric literals in any rendering path, so a retyped constant fails rather than passes; and a regression test for each of the six, so none can recur silently.
